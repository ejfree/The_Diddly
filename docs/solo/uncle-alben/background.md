# Uncle Alben — Background

## Who He Is

A mountain of a man in scarred lamellar, a brandy-cask holy symbol swinging from his belt, Uncle Alben blesses his allies with a swig of his own rambutan brandy — "Rambui" to everyone who's ever shared a cup with him — before a fight, and beats the mercy of Cayden Cailean into anyone who threatens his flock.

Loud, warm, generous to a fault, and slightly drunk at all times — but never too drunk to smash a skull with a maul or knit a friend's ribs back together. Everyone in the party just calls him Uncle Alben.

He is an **orc**, specifically a **Rainfall Orc** — born in a rain forest, raised amid tangled trees and torrential storms, and adept at moving through jungle terrain and shrugging off the ailments common to humid environs. Before he ever picked up a maul in the name of his god, he worked as a **barkeep**: hefting barrels, polishing steins, holding his liquor, and rowdily socializing his way through a career defined by conversation as much as strength.

## Faith: Why an Orc Worships an Outsider God

Orc worship of their own pantheon is actually uncommon in Golarion lore — most orc deities are grim, war-obsessed Belkzen gods whom orcs tend to see as just another target worth challenging rather than reverent figures. Less violent orc holds often adopt outside gods instead (Sarenrae is the classic example).

Alben is cut from that same cloth. He took one look at the dour, lightning-throwing, grudge-keeping Belkzen pantheon and decided he'd rather follow a god who throws a party. **Cayden Cailean's** creed of freedom, courage, and generosity suited him a lot better than a childhood spent being told to hate everything with a face.

Cayden Cailean — "The Accidental God" — is himself proof that divinity doesn't require solemnity. One of the Ascended, a former sellsword who stumbled into the Starstone Cathedral on a dare during a night of riotous celebration and emerged three days later, laughing, still intoxicated, and having achieved godhood. He never stopped enjoying a good drink, defending the vulnerable, or roaming in search of adventure — which is more or less Alben's own life philosophy, just scaled up to divine proportions.

Alben's edicts, by extension of his faith, are simple: **drink, aid the oppressed, seek glory and adventure.** His anathema is just as plainly stated: **don't waste alcohol, don't be mean or standoffish when drunk, and never oppress the vulnerable.** He follows all three without apparent effort — they're less commandments to him than just... who he already was.

He carries his faith physically as well as spiritually: a tankard is Cayden Cailean's sacred symbol, and Alben's own holy symbol takes the shape of a brandy cask, swinging from his belt alongside his maul.

## Rambui: The Orcish Brew

Rambutan brandy is not Alben's invention. It's a staple across the jungle orc tribes he comes from, brewed wherever rambutan trees grow wild and the fruit falls thick enough to ferment by the barrelful. Every hold has its own recipe, its own barrel, and its own firm opinion about whose batch is the roughest — which, among orcs, is the highest compliment a drink can earn.

The name is no accident, and neither is the flavor. Generations back, some orc trader got hold of a bottle of a smooth, honeyed, foreign spirit called Drambuie — refined, mellow, the kind of thing meant to be sipped slowly and respected. The jungle tribes took one taste and decided that smooth and mellow was the entire problem. So they set about fixing it. **Rambui** is what happens when a proud, deliberately unrefined culture takes everything restrained and pleasant about that original spirit and exaggerates every rough edge on purpose: harsher on the way in, harder on the way down, and engineered — not accidental — to leave a hangover worth bragging about the next morning. It is, by design, a joke at the original's expense, and the orcs who brew it wear that joke as a point of pride rather than an insult. A proper batch of Rambui should make a civilized drinker's eyes water and an orc grin.

Alben makes his own — his personal recipe, tuned over years, the batch he's proudest of and shares most freely (see [inventory.md](inventory.md) and [liquor.md](liquor.md)). But he's also a collector at heart: wherever his travels take him through orc holds and jungle settlements, he seeks out the local Rambui, trades for it, samples it, argues good-naturedly about whose is worse, and packs a bottle away in his Spacious Pouches for later. His stock isn't one recipe — it's a running tally of every rough, proud, deliberately-terrible batch he's ever been poured, with his own brew simply being the one he trusts most.

## Solomon

Alben's constant companion is **Solomon**, a mandarin duck of unreasonable dignity. Where Alben is loud, Solomon is composed. Where Alben sweats brandy, Solomon smells faintly of clean feathers and quiet mornings. The contrast is, presumably, the point — every rowdy tavern brawl or battlefield charge is undercut by the small, poised bird riding along for the whole thing without so much as ruffling a feather.

### The Palanquin

Solomon does not simply perch on Alben's shoulder — he **rides**. Mounted there is a small, elegant structure built to Solomon's exact scale: a miniature palanquin, no bigger than a jewelry box, with curved supports, a fringed little canopy, and cushioned seating that would look at home carrying visiting nobility rather than a duck. It's absurd. It's also unmistakably dignified, in the specific way that only something this small and this ornate, worn without irony by something enormous and battle-scarred, can be. Where the rest of Alben looks like it survived several fights, Solomon's palanquin looks like it has never once been in danger — and somehow, so far, that's remained true.

### The Collar of Empathy

Solomon wears a **collar of empathy** — a slim band of tooled leather and quietly glowing crystal fitted snug around his neck, connected to Alben. It carries no words and no clear thoughts, only *feeling*: the shape of a room's mood, the flicker of fear behind a stranger's shoulders, the difference between someone about to throw a punch and someone about to break down and cry. Alben, for all his volume, has never been especially subtle on his own — Solomon is how he reads a room he'd otherwise barrel straight through.

It works both ways. When Alben is furious, Solomon goes rigid and silent in the palanquin. When Alben is grieving — which he hides better than most people realize — Solomon presses close against his collar and doesn't move, and something in Alben's shoulders always eases, just slightly, when he does. Neither of them has ever needed to explain this arrangement to the other. It simply is how they work.

The contrast between them — one an oversized, roaring engine of violence and mercy, the other a small, composed passenger who quietly tells him what he can't always feel for himself — is, presumably, the point.

### How They Met

Solomon did not come from a breeder, a market stall, or a gift from some grateful patron. He came from the worst night of Alben's bartending career, back before the maul and the holy symbol, when Alben still measured his worth in poured drinks and settled tabs rather than swings of a warhammer.

There was a regular — an older, soft-spoken merchant who came in every few nights, always alone, always with Solomon riding on his shoulder in that same little palanquin. The man rarely talked business or gossip like the rest of the bar; mostly he just sat, nursed one drink slowly, and murmured quietly to the duck between sips. Alben liked him. Quiet regulars were easy regulars, and this one tipped well and never started trouble.

One night the man didn't come in alone in the usual sense — he came in already unwell, pale and short of breath in a way Alben, focused on a packed bar and a dozen other tabs, didn't clock quickly enough. By the time Alben noticed something was wrong, the man was slumping sideways off his stool. Alben caught him before he hit the floor, but it was already too late; the man's heart simply stopped, right there against Alben's chest, with Solomon shrieking and flapping at both of them, frantic in a way Alben had never seen the composed little bird behave.

Alben did what he always did in a crisis — he acted first and grieved later. He laid the man out on the bar, checked for breath, pounded on a chest that wouldn't answer, and shouted for anyone who knew healing. Nobody in the bar that night did. Solomon never left the man's side, standing on his still chest, collar glowing faintly, as if trying to push some scrap of feeling into a body that could no longer receive it.

The man died before a real healer arrived. Alben closed the bar early that night for the first time in years. He sat with the body, and with the duck, until the temple wagon came to collect him in the morning — and Solomon, when Alben finally reached out to gently move him off the man's chest, simply climbed onto Alben's shoulder instead and stayed there. He hasn't willingly left it since.

Nobody came forward to claim Solomon. No family showed up asking after the collar, the palanquin, or the bird. Alben asked around for weeks, half out of duty and half hoping someone would take the responsibility off his hands — he'd never owned so much as a barroom cat, let alone something magical and clearly grieving. No one ever came. Eventually Alben stopped asking and started listening instead, and the collar of empathy did what it was apparently built to do: it told him, in feeling rather than words, that Solomon had chosen him back.

Alben has never been entirely sure whether Solomon picked him because he was the last kind hand the old merchant's duck had known, or because the collar sensed something in Alben worth attaching to before Alben himself had found it. He suspects, some nights over a cup of Rambui, that it might have been both. He never did learn the merchant's name. He never needed to — Solomon remembered enough for the two of them, and eventually, so did he.

## Voice & Presence

Everything about Alben is oversized: his frame, his warmth, his generosity, his volume. He is the kind of person who blesses people with alcohol before violence and heals their ribs with the same casual confidence. He does not do anything by half-measures — drinking, fighting, or looking after the people he's decided are his to protect.

"Flock" is the operative word for how he thinks about the people around him. He's a barkeep and a cleric both, and the two callings blend seamlessly: pour the drink, hear the trouble, and if trouble comes looking for his people, answer it with a swing of the maul and a follow-up mercy of healing.
